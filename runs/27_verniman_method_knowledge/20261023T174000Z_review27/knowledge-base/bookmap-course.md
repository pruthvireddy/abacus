# Bookmap educational course selected by Vern

The [Bookmap Basic page](https://verniman.blogspot.com/2012/06/bookmap-basic.html) contains four third-party Bookmap lessons. These are course teachings selected by Vern, not first-person evidence that Vern applies every illustrated setup. The presenter introduces himself as Bruce Pringle (caption spelling). All retained captions are English and automatically generated. Exact prices, names and specialized terms may be mistranscribed. This note interprets spoken descriptions; it does not claim video-frame inspection.

## Part 1 — Mechanics before signals

[Lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3m_Stn4hxCQ) · full retained captions reviewed, 0:01–1:07:51.

At **4:50–7:10**, the presenter distinguishes resting limit orders, the best bid/offer, market aggressors and the current versus historical book. At **10:30–17:50**, he layers candles, historical best bid/offer, trade dots and historical depth. Trade-dot aggregation changes with screen zoom; that graphical aggregation is distinct from the recorded underlying trades. Historical depth matters because it preserves the prior book state a trader otherwise would have to remember.

**18:50–26:40** separates the trade that consumes liquidity from changes to the liquidity available. In an illustrative sequence, bids pull before a small sell trades several ticks lower. This is more precise than reading the blog's categorical “orders move price” sentence as though all price movement required a large aggressive trade. The course explicitly describes the last transaction staying unchanged while the best bid moves after cancellations.

**30:20–38:50** explains a sweep as an aggressor consuming multiple price levels. It can lead to continuation, a pullback, a failed break or a book flip. A sweep is not inherently a stop run; that qualification is repeated in Q&A at **57:10–57:50**. At **37:10–38:30**, a failed downward sweep that returns into the prior range illustrates rejection rather than acceptance below it. At **38:00–38:50**, former bid-side liquidity appearing on the offer supplies the book-flip interpretation.

At **39:00–46:30**, absorption means resting orders take the aggressive flow without allowing the anticipated further progress. High aggressive buying at resistance can therefore support a bearish reading if it fails to lift price. The relevant observation is the interaction and result, not the color or size of a dot alone. **46:30–53:20** distinguishes exhaustion: the aggressor's participation fades. It does not require low resting liquidity, and exhaustion alone does not prove an opposite-side initiative.

**53:20–56:10** makes instrument behavior part of the exercise: compare liquidity, volatility, speed, time of day and retests; mark many examples and use replay. **1:04:40–1:06:20** cautions that a strong sweep may never return to its origin. The method must accommodate a missed entry instead of assuming every attractive level receives a fill.

**Abacus synthesis.** Keep four questions separate: what caused a change in the book, what actually traded, whether the new price area gained acceptance, and what action has a defined risk boundary. These are observational and decision layers; no course illustration establishes measured expectancy. No universal numerical threshold for absorption or exhaustion is supplied here.

## Part 2 — Put transactions inside an auction structure

[Lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ixo_DoBGYko) · full retained captions reviewed, 0:01–52:18.

**8:30–14:20** distinguishes two contextual roles for visible depth. Longer-standing liquidity helps delineate the current auction and can attract price as a place where trading can occur. Newly appearing size near price can introduce an imbalance and push other participants away. These are the presenter's interpretations of behavior, not a deterministic rule that old liquidity always attracts and new liquidity always repels. He specifically asks whether liquidity stays, moves or disappears as price approaches.

At **14:20–19:50**, balance means a price area in which transactions and time accumulate, with responsive selling above and responsive buying below. The single distribution, POC, high-volume nodes, low-volume edges and VWAP describe different properties. The course distinguishes VWAP from POC; it does not specify a value-area calculation. At **20:00–22:50**, b- and p-shaped profiles are explicitly contextual. A lower-volume distribution alone does not prove continuation down; the composition of actual buying/selling and the higher timeframe may support another reading.

**23:00–28:10** uses failed excursions and smaller ranges nested within a larger range. A larger balanced day can contain several shifts of local acceptance. At **25:40–26:50**, the presenter repeats an approximately 80% claim about testing the opposite side after returning into a range, attributed to what he has heard from traders. No dataset, sample definition, range definition or cost treatment is given. **This is hearsay teaching, not an estimated probability established by this research.**

**28:10–39:40** contrasts an excursion with initiative that breaks a range, trades beyond it and continues to support the new area. Repeated relocation produces multiple volume distributions. A large trade-dot cluster in a short breakout need not make that breakout price the high-volume node of the eventual range: cumulative trading inside the accepted area can be larger. **39:40–44:20** combines structural channels and relative swing strength. Stronger moves may produce shallower pullbacks; a full retest is a possibility, not a condition guaranteed by the market.

The exercise at **44:20–46:20** is sequential: analyze one marked example thoroughly, then compare variations in another. **46:00–47:00** emphasizes the historical zone of activity rather than interpreting one bright line in isolation. **49:50–50:55** explicitly allows a developing opposite-side response to alter the anticipated pullback/continuation.

**Abacus synthesis.** “Acceptance” in this course combines time, transactions and the response after a break. It remains qualitative: the lesson gives no fixed duration, volume fraction or maximum return distance. The research benefit is a conditional framework, not a programmable definition.

## Part 3 — Three executions with different premises

[Lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Au20OwdAG5s) · full retained captions reviewed, 0:00–58:38.

**2:40–8:40** supplies the analysis order: mark higher-timeframe levels with a method the trader understands; inspect current structure within those levels; then examine liquidity and transactions. The presenter permits chart, profile, fundamental or other macro methods. His demonstration uses horizontal and diagonal levels rather than claiming that one indicator is essential. The starting point is context, not the isolated trade dot.

**Reversal, 8:40–22:50.** The narrated example first appears bearish: the lower-timeframe structure has broken down and offers press lower. But it reaches a premarked higher-timeframe area, selling becomes sparse at the extreme, price returns into the prior structure, bids rise, offers withdraw, and initiative buying breaks the local structure. The source therefore waits for a change in evidence. A touch of the higher-timeframe level alone is insufficient. A conservative entry seeks a return toward the break-origin zone; a stronger V-shaped move may offer only a shallower micro-range test. The source discusses layered orders and, for sufficiently fast or automated traders, joining initiative directly. The proposed stop is below the initiative area. Targets include persistent opposing liquidity and, in that example, the current range's VWAP. Taking partials before displayed liquidity is an execution suggestion, not a universal two-tick rule.

**Momentum, 22:50–28:50.** This can be a fresh entry or an addition after partial profits from the reversal. The premise is already-supported higher structure, limited selling on the pullback, rising bids/pulling offers and substantial buying that lifts price into another accepted range. The presenter describes a market entry after that initiative becomes visible, with the stop below it. This differs from placing a passive order at the full pullback location: an effective momentum move may not revisit that price. The later retrospective high is illustration, not evidence that the entire path was forecast or captured.

**Pullback continuation, 33:10–42:20.** First a range breaks and directional aggressors trade beyond it. The new cluster's aggressor balance should agree with the break. Then a low-volume counter-move returns toward a low-volume zone near the origin. The lesson discusses placing a limit order early, allowing for a front-run because little may trade at the exact preferred price. It offers several risk variants: an aggressive stop just back inside the prior range; a wider stop beyond its POC; or a partial entry with another planned entry near that POC. These are alternatives with different exposure, not interchangeable settings. Any future implementation must freeze one before testing.

**42:20–46:10** requires study of the actual context and its variations rather than memorization of the ideal diagram. **49:40–53:20** is especially valuable: if aggressive buying instead charges back upward against the short premise, the presenter may cancel the resting order or exit before the stop is reached. A gentle, low-volume retracement is treated differently. **53:40–55:30** also distinguishes initial absorption from later trading through the same liquidity. The first bounce does not prove permanent defense.

**Abacus synthesis.** The three entry methods answer different questions: has a reversal acquired support, is initiative sufficiently strong to join, or is an already-established move offering a retracement? Mixing their permissions after entry destroys the distinction. “Contextual” means updating against observable disconfirmation; it does not grant unlimited discretion to move a stop farther away.

## Part 4 — Confluence should refine a setup

[Lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tl85HACmwnw) · full retained captions reviewed, 0:01–59:26.

The explicit constraint at **3:40–4:40** is that CVD, iceberg detection and large-lot displays add information at a setup location; they are not independent entry signals. **4:40–8:20** revisits a possible reversal before several conditions have appeared. The presenter lists the missing structure break, higher bids, withdrawing offers and initiative buying, preserving the distinction between early evidence and completed setup.

**8:20–16:40** layers CVD divergence, a detected iceberg and larger pending-order estimates onto the later-confirmed reversal. The spoken CVD example alternates “higher lows” and “higher high”; the safe abstraction is weakening sell-side pressure relative to price's lower low, rather than an exact caption-derived formula. The iceberg tool is described as comparing displayed quantity with actual transactions. The large-lot tracker is explicitly an approximation based on order-arrival behavior and a configurable threshold. A large displayed order may also disappear. Neither device identifies an institution or proves motive. The assumed data/feed model and version matter if these observations are ever operationalized.

**18:30–22:50** demonstrates the historical API execution tools. Chase trails the chosen reference; Escape moves away if supporting order-book conditions are absent; Execute reprices a limit order aggressively when a configured imbalance occurs. “Execute” is not a prediction of a good trade. At **33:10–34:20**, the presenter says earlier automated entry can be caught if the opposing aggressor appears. Treat these as historical feature descriptions, not verified instructions for a current Bookmap release.

**24:10–31:40** shows split aggressor-volume columns, optional resets, filtered time and sales, imbalance measures and CVD. The reset location changes the measurement window. The illustrated 10-lot filter and observed imbalance percentage are example settings, not Vern's permanent thresholds. In the pullback example at **36:20–39:50**, several confluences are weak or absent. The lesson does not require every indicator to agree on every trade.

**39:50–43:25** compares ES and Nasdaq movement as additional context, including the possibility that the related market presents the clearer opportunity. The presenter explicitly says correlations shift. Proposed cross-market trades or hedges are possibilities, not tested hedge ratios or stable predictive lead/lag relationships.

**43:25–47:30** returns to actual fill concerns and alternative stops. The speaker briefly mixes up Chase/Escape/Execute names before correcting himself around **46:40–47:15**. Do not convert that passage into a precise current configuration recipe. **47:30–50:30** advocates mastering one setup and one useful confluence before adding more. Zoom and settings should serve that task; more indicators can overwhelm the decision.

## A precise reading sequence

| Layer | Question | Evidence versus inference |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Which higher-timeframe and local range boundaries were known already? | Record the level and availability; do not redraw it around the eventual reversal. |
| Book | Where is liquidity staying, shifting, adding or withdrawing? | Displayed quantity is observable; intentions and identity are interpretations. |
| Transactions | What crosses the spread, and with what price result? | Trade size/color alone is incomplete; continued progress or failure is part of the reading. |
| Structure | Does price return into the old area or establish another? | The course uses qualitative time/volume acceptance; a test needs an explicit definition. |
| Setup | Reversal, initiative/momentum, or pullback continuation? | Keep each premise, entry method and invalidation distinct. |
| Execution | Can the proposed price fill; how much risk exists if it does? | Front-running, crossing and waiting exchange fill likelihood against entry quality. |
| Update | What new evidence contradicts the premise? | Opposite initiative can invalidate; a further target reached later cannot repair an invalid entry. |

## What this course still does not establish

The lessons supply observational examples and discretionary setup families. They do not provide a complete trade log, fees, queue position, slippage, drawdown distribution, independently measured win rate or a universal calibration. Claims such as “high probability” remain presenter descriptions. Absorption, exhaustion, significance of size, higher-timeframe context and acceptance need frozen causal definitions before an empirical Abacus study. Transcript review does not verify the order book's historical completeness or the presenter's inferred trader motives.
