# Scalping — three speakers, several different games

Vern's [Scalping Basics](https://verniman.blogspot.com/2013/08/scalping-tape.html) page embeds three substantial lessons: [FuturesTrader71 with Big Mike](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU), [Peter Davies with futures.io](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk), and [John Grady of No BS Day Trading with Big Mike](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k). Grady's webinar is sponsored by Jigsaw, as its introduction states. These speakers explain their own experience and preferences. Vern's selection of them does not make their distinct definitions, equipment, risk practices or product recommendations one unified personal trading plan.

## 1. Define the game before carrying a rule across it

The speakers do not use *scalping* identically.

| Speaker | Principal game illustrated | Relevant decision horizon |
|---|---|---|
| FT71 | Small rotations, continuation/failure patterns and actively managed multi-entry campaigns | Short rotations within the intraday auction; prior session, profile and initial-balance references can matter |
| Davies | Spread capture and market-making techniques; also pauses within a directional leg and temporary countertrend spikes | The current local activity and related markets, often seconds rather than a whole-session forecast |
| Grady | Joining immediate pressure, seeking the next several ticks and responding when that pressure changes | Current executions and context, with the expected opportunity changing as the day's action changes |

FT71 describes nibbling small rotations and emphasizes limits on time, size and permitted circumstances ([03:43–07:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=223s), [30:03–32:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=1803s)). Davies deliberately returns to buying the bid and selling the offer, while allowing larger returns when the immediate opportunity supports them ([01:53–03:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=113s)). Grady says his own scalps are often aimed at more than a single tick ([22:15–24:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=1335s)).

Consequently, Davies's rejection of volume profile or cumulative delta for **his spread-capture examples** ([1:19:07–1:19:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=4747s), [1:23:48–1:24:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=5028s)) does not refute FT71's use of profile context. Grady also qualifies his DOM preference as personal rather than claiming nobody can use footprints ([1:21:08–1:22:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=4868s)). They are observing different opportunities through different displays.

## 2. Small gross targets make execution economics central

FT71's discussion of membership, exchange fees and turnover shows why fixed costs consume a large proportion of a small target ([19:30–25:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=1170s)). Davies similarly compares all-in cost with tick value ([04:00–05:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=240s), [10:22–13:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=622s)). Those historical prices, membership programs, tick specifications and broker arrangements were not verified as current. They illustrate a dependency; they are not an up-to-date cost sheet.

Davies's anecdote about an 85% winning scalper explicitly excludes orders that were placed but never filled. It is an attributed anecdote, not Vern's win rate or a validated dataset. High win frequency alone says little without the average gain, average loss, scratch costs and rare adverse moves. His thin-market example also describes several attempts before a fill ([54:48–58:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=3288s)).

**Abacus interpretation:** record filled trades and unfilled/canceled opportunities separately. A fill-only record conditions on which orders were executed; the unfilled orders may have been the most favorable opportunities. A scratch at entry still pays costs. Reliable expectancy requires actual realized returns after costs, not the displayed proportion of winning trades or a gross target assumed filled whenever price touched it.

The learning benefit the speakers seek is repeated observation and feedback. More repetitions do not automatically improve profitability, and transactions from the same session are not necessarily independent statistical samples. Their coaching claims about learning faster are not evidence that a beginner will be profitable within a specified number of weeks.

## 3. Queue position and matching rules are part of the opportunity

Davies's slow-market *crocodile* example tries to improve queue position while retaining sufficient displayed liquidity behind the order. Before accepting a fill, the trader reassesses whether to cancel or remain ([18:58–23:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=1138s)). The premise is not simply that a large queue provides permanent protection. Displayed orders can disappear; being filled immediately because all preceding bids vanish may signal an adverse situation ([1:20:06–1:21:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=4806s)).

He explains that allocation is not always first-in-first-out and gives a historical Treasury matching example. The exact current product algorithm and data-feed treatment remain outside this study. Do not implement the narrated quantities as current exchange rules. A platform's estimated queue position and a data feed's order-level information also are different evidence.

His examples sometimes submit a replacement before canceling the previous order because he expects the old one will not fill ([35:55–36:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=2155s), [49:26–50:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=2966s)). That is a description of his discretionary handling, not a guarantee that both cannot execute. A reproducible order policy would have to specify outstanding exposure, cancellation acknowledgments and partial fills.

## 4. Related markets help identify the current pressure

Davies resets/recenters related DOMs to a common observation moment, then compares excursions and one-sided executions. A thinner product can move while a thicker product primarily shows trading into one side. They are not supposed to move identical tick distances ([14:02–18:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=842s)). He rejects a market that **always** leads or lags ([1:17:56–1:19:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=4676s)).

The related-market read serves both entry and exit. His mid-leg example joins renewed pressure after a pause instead of requiring a large chart pullback; later stalled price and adverse behavior in related instruments weaken the trade ([45:47–53:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=2747s)). He acknowledges that he stayed in that example longer than was ideal for a scalper. Preserving this qualification avoids turning a selected profitable sequence into a perfect textbook execution.

The [audio/correlation synthesis](video-audio-and-correlations.md) develops the same distinction between local agreement and a predictive leader. Neither a chart comparison nor these selected examples establish a coefficient, causal mechanism or latency-adjusted trading edge.

## 5. Size matters through its interaction with price

Grady's core contrast is between quiet trading with little directional pressure and a sustained sequence of aggressive trades that consumes successive levels. His ES narration compares a thin midday move with a later drive through the day's lows ([1:00:59–1:10:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=3659s)). His thesis is to ask **how** a price was reached, what size traded, and whether subsequent activity continues in that direction. A support label on a chart is insufficient if buying does not actually stop further decline ([51:22–54:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=3082s)).

Grady also says a large isolated print may stop rather than extend the move; total activity and the surrounding scenario matter more than filtering only huge individual orders ([1:23:01–1:25:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=4981s), [1:34:29–1:35:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=5669s)). His two-person buyer-versus-seller story is explicitly a teaching simplification. Public prints do not reveal whether one person or many traded, whether positions opened or closed, or whose account was forced out. His stop-run and short-covering narratives remain interpretations of the sequence, not identified participant records.

Davies warns against automatically buying in front of any large bid. One narrated large order withdraws; another example has neighboring bids grow, actual volume execute at the reference, and support follow the market upward ([38:59–45:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=2339s)). The distinction he wants is a developing story rather than a single large number. Neither age nor size guarantees that an order remains. Cancellation alone does not establish manipulative intent, even where the speaker labels an order fake or a spoof.

## 6. “Getting the edge” is an execution phrase, not a risk-free claim

Grady uses *getting the edge* for entering as transactions clear a level, after which the inside market moves favorably and a scratch appears readily available ([37:44–40:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=2264s)). It is an immediate favorable position in the bid/offer sequence. It is different from establishing a positive long-run expected return.

His language about a free look or worst-case breakeven is not literally guaranteed. Displayed liquidity can cancel, a queue can prevent an exit, a market can jump, and costs remain. He later explicitly acknowledges that following pressure can sell the bottom or produce only a tiny move; risk parameters are necessary ([1:13:07–1:14:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=4387s)). The note adopts that uncertainty rather than the earlier absolute phrase.

Davies likewise describes actively scratching rather than placing a one-tick automatic stop in his examples ([29:25–31:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=1765s)). He mentions an emergency protective stop while describing his own practice. This does not provide a general substitute for a defined maximum loss or permission for indefinite adverse exposure. Grady's requested detailed risk questions are explicitly deferred by the host ([1:34:12–1:34:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=viBGTEGFF1k&t=5652s)); the lesson is therefore not a complete risk specification.

## 7. FT71 separates fixed boundaries from flexible execution

FT71's *sandbox* specifies the session/time, permitted conditions, size and relevant observation period. He emphasizes particular care around the open and news, where larger-horizon activity can overwhelm the small rotation being traded ([30:03–32:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=1803s)). Within those boundaries his execution is fluid, but violating exits or allowing one large loss to consume many small gains breaks the business model ([46:35–51:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=2795s)).

His **regressive sizing** example reduces contract count as the day approaches its loss limit, then restores size with a lag during recovery ([51:05–58:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=3065s)). Four contracts, a $1,200 daily limit and quarter-limit increments are instructional historical numbers, not Vern's plan. The spoken recovery thresholds are insufficiently clear to reconstruct a unique state machine. The supported principle is lower exposure during deterioration; it is not doubling to recover losses. The speaker's use of *anti-Martingale* while discussing increasing size after losses is also loose terminology, not adopted as a formal definition here.

A later passage discusses an advanced, preplanned *feeler* and adding/removing size around a consolidation ([1:11:46–1:14:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=4306s)). It includes adding after adverse movement, while explicitly distinguishing the intended method from uncontrolled averaging. This tension must remain visible: the captions do not supply complete allowable price distances, maximum cumulative exposure or invalidation conditions. A reader cannot safely turn the label “planned” into a mechanical authorization to keep adding.

His Q&A about reducing an already troubled position is an attempt to limit damage and restore perspective ([58:34–1:01:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=3514s)). It does not remove the earlier requirement to honor the trade's limits, and a scalp should not silently become a swing trade because it is losing.

## 8. The replay demonstrates a process and its limitations

FT71's accelerated replay starts with unsuccessful long and short attempts. He repeatedly notes the missing usual context: prior-day levels, profile, initial balance, rotation statistics and familiar tools ([1:17:26–1:24:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=4646s), [1:29:32–1:31:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=5372s)). Those failures are part of the evidence. His later campaign appears to recover earlier losses in the narrated example, but that one sequence does not validate the strategy or imply that a later winner will repair a series of feeler losses.

He defines a trade for his analyzer as **flat to flat**, including all additions and partial exits. The theoretical average incorporates realized gains within the still-open campaign ([1:35:15–1:37:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=5715s)). A campaign's scratch price differs from the fill average of remaining contracts. The tool's aggregate MFE/MAE can also count contract-ticks: two contracts moving two ticks give four contract-ticks ([1:16:20–1:17:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=4580s)). Those units cannot be compared directly with a per-contract price excursion. See the [risk-management review](video-ft71-risk-management.md) for whole-campaign breakeven and costs.

His narrated impulse/correction and continuation tendencies, including a “70ish percent” claim ([1:31:19–1:33:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=5479s)), are not reproducible here: the segmentation rule, observations and test are absent. Fixed historical ES point distances are likewise not current volatility parameters.

## 9. Feedback must retain actual outcomes

FT71 proposes tracking realized expectancy, durations, MAE/MFE, scale-outs, time of day, drawdown and execution errors ([1:41:56–1:47:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PmQCAJecfuU&t=6116s)). An error is departure from the intended process, even when it wins. His client example removes error trades and substitutes planned losses to construct a better-looking counterfactual. That can motivate a diagnostic question, but it is not the client's actual return or a proven attainable improvement. Keep the full original record, labels and hypothetical adjustments separate.

MFE divided by MAE is also not the same quantity as realized expectancy or a return measured against a predeclared R. The presentation's neighboring discussion of these metrics does not make them interchangeable. Optimize only after defining comparable units, campaign boundaries and the fill/cost model; the small replay's displayed expectancy is not a stable estimate.

Davies warns that spread-capture simulation needs realistic queue and fill handling and that accelerated replay does not preserve the same feel ([57:46–58:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=3466s), [1:08:34–1:09:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=4114s)). His preference for a named historical demo provider is not independent validation of that simulator or current alternatives. His further opinion that these discretionary trades need no backtest ([1:12:48–1:13:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QJH-zli3AZk&t=4368s)) is not adopted as an evidence exemption: prospective records, defined risk and execution-faithful evaluation still matter.

## Remaining implementation boundaries

The shared lesson is to match the decision horizon to the actual opportunity, learn normal activity before labeling an exception, pay attention to executions and response, and keep losses and operational failures within declared limits. The speakers disagree over thinner versus thicker markets, the usefulness of charts, preferred observation baselines and precise opportunities. Those are attributed choices, not contradictions that require inventing a consensus setup.

Historical computer/network/security advice, fee/membership terms, exchange rules, product availability, broker claims and HFT-market-share anecdotes are contextual statements of their time. They were not checked as current operational instructions. In particular, disabling protective computer software is not a requirement abstracted into this knowledge base. No numerical rule, specific platform, account size or live-trading exercise was adopted or executed for this review.
