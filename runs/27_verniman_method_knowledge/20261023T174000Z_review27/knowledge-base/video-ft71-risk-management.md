## 1. Risk has several distinct layers

FT71 describes emotional capital as confidence built by repeating the planned process, rather than merely accumulating winning P&L ([q_d, 03:01–06:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=181s)). Both a frightening loss and excessive confidence after a streak can disrupt that process ([37:39–39:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=2259s)). These are his experiential psychological observations, not diagnoses or measured causal effects.

His practical risk structure separates:

- **Trade invalidation:** where the auction hypothesis no longer makes sense.
- **Position size:** how much financial exposure that structural distance creates.
- **Setup-attempt limit:** how many attempts at the same idea he permits.
- **Daily loss limit:** when trading must stop for the day.

At [39:44–45:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=2384s), he describes a stop as the planned admission cost for testing an idea. Importantly, his three failed same-direction attempts retire **that setup/direction**; he explicitly says this does not necessarily end his day. His daily loss limit is a separate gate. This differs from Vern's own trading-plan statement about stopping after three consecutive losers.

At [1:41:05–1:43:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=6065s), structural invalidation comes before deciding the acceptable contract count. A larger necessary stop does not authorize proportionally larger account risk. His historical capitalization and broker-margin examples ([16:20–20:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=980s)) illustrate leverage; quoted margin dollars are not maximum trade loss or current broker requirements.

## 2. Information and price have a tradeoff

At [08:16–14:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=496s), he contrasts accepting uncertainty at a planned favorable location with waiting for additional information while the available price moves away. He calls these information risk and price risk. For his style, entry near a well-defined wrong point can be preferable to waiting until another trader's first profit-taking location.

This does not establish that all confirmation is harmful. It describes the cost of waiting in his framework. A specified order-flow trigger, such as those in the [value-transition videos](video-value-transition.md), can still be part of a plan. “More information” is not equivalent to certainty, and a late entry must be evaluated using its actual remaining target distance and stop.

At [1:23:30–1:28:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6qkzPYIxonI&t=5010s), he makes the sequence explicit: develop auction context and a hypothesis; identify a substantial reference to lean against; then compare attainable target distance with invalidation distance. His illustrative six-tick upside/twelve-tick downside requires a high win rate just to break even before costs. It is an example of the arithmetic, not a recommended configuration.

## 3. Scaling changes the whole-position economics

At [1:17:17–1:22:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=4637s), FT71 favors entering and taking partial exits because it fits his psychology. He does not claim it universally maximizes profits: he distinguishes the smoother experience he seeks from a mechanical all-in/all-out alternative.

His **theoretical average** is the exit price on the remaining position that would offset realized gains/losses from the same trade campaign. It differs from the remaining units' original fill average. His simple long example starts with two ES contracts at 1400, one exits at 1401, and one remains. Before costs, the remaining contract could exit at 1399 for the combined trade to scratch: the realized one-point gain offsets its one-point loss.

He also discusses moving that remaining stop to 1397 as an arithmetic illustration of preserving the original combined loss allowance, **then explicitly says that is not how he trades** ([1:20:01–1:20:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=4801s)). Do not turn this what-if calculation into permission to widen stops. His actual first-scale location depends on the auction, not a fixed one-point rule.

At [1:35:36–1:40:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=5736s), his scratch decision also depends on sequence: reaching a predefined scale area and then returning to entry differs from an adverse move that merely comes back to the original fill while the hypothesis remains intact. He names opening swing, midpoint and profile references. A stop change is thus intended to express an altered trade situation, rather than relief at seeing zero P&L.

**Abacus boundary:** whole-campaign breakeven does not remove exposure, profit giveback, costs or adverse execution. His personal active/manual-stop practice is described at [1:37:57–1:38:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=5877s), where he also says he does not advocate abandoning protective stops. That personal practice cannot become a general rule for this knowledge base.

## 4. Expectancy is a diagnostic decomposition

At [10:01–13:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6qkzPYIxonI&t=601s), FT71 decomposes average return into win frequency/average win and loss frequency/average loss. He prefers consistent ticks or points during review because dollar attachment can distract from execution quality. The components help distinguish small winners from frequent losses; win percentage alone cannot settle profitability.

For an Abacus record, the units must also specify whether returns are per contract, per round trip or per complete campaign. Variable sizes and partial exits otherwise make comparisons misleading. Realized fills already include their execution prices; commissions/exchange fees still need explicit deduction. At [38:25–40:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6qkzPYIxonI&t=2305s), he clarifies that his example includes live fill slippage but initially excludes fees. His historical net-dollar arithmetic and suggested tick targets are not current cost assumptions or universal performance standards.

He discusses MAE, MFE and time between trades ([q_d, 1:03:34–1:16:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=3814s)). These help investigate how much favorable/adverse movement a trade experienced and whether rapid re-entry or long hesitation accompanied it. His client examples suggest possible execution problems; the statistics alone cannot prove fear, distinguish entry quality from exit behavior, or establish an ideal waiting time.

## 5. Fix one part of the process long enough to observe it

The second webinar's core exercise ([20:09–29:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6qkzPYIxonI&t=1209s)) uses a chosen entry style plus a stable management policy: stop, scaling approach, target rules and treatment of runners. He asks for at least thirty repetitions, records deviations, and starts a new sample when management changes. He later mentions fifty as preferable. These are his coaching batch sizes, not a statistical guarantee.

His five execution-error classes are:

1. Omitting a planned signal through hesitation.
2. Entering, then changing the prescribed management.
3. Chasing a planned entry after the location has gone.
4. Platform/order mistakes such as wrong side or quantity.
5. Impulsive trades with no setup.

At [1:06:29–1:13:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6qkzPYIxonI&t=3989s), he prioritizes a trade-by-trade execution journal over a bare P&L diary. Record errors even on winners. Separate execution-as-planned, early stop, moved stop and adjusted target; group comparable setups afterward. At [50:34–52:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6qkzPYIxonI&t=3034s), studying what happened after an early exit is a later diagnostic step, once reliable execution records exist.

**Abacus interpretation:** retain all trades and the original error labels. Showing an error-free subset can diagnose a process; deleting error trades from the reported actual record would overstate attainable results. A missed signal also needs its own opportunity record rather than silently disappearing from the denominator.

## 6. The simulation exercise is behavioral, not proof of free expectancy

At [1:00:32–1:11:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=3632s), he describes a **simulation-only** coin-toss exercise: randomize direction, use fixed management, complete repetitions and observe the response to wins/losses. He compares small samples with different targets and partial exits. His spoken positive-expectancy example illustrates his argument for management, but no retained workbook, randomization sequence, full fill model or independent replication validates it here. Captions also contain inconsistent aggregate tick arithmetic. It does not prove that random entries plus scaling generate a durable positive expectancy after costs.

In the later webinar ([54:07–56:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6qkzPYIxonI&t=3247s)), he describes a one-tick target penalty for his simulator's fills. That is a historical platform-specific training approximation. It does not establish a general model of queue position, stop slippage or live execution. No exercise or live trade was run for this study.

## 7. Important limits on the speakers' probability language

FT71 repeatedly says that an unknown next trade must be 50/50 ([q_d, 14:26–15:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=866s), [49:01–57:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=2941s)). **That does not follow mathematically.** Two possible outcomes and uncertainty about which will occur do not imply equal probabilities. His defensible practical lesson is accepting loss as possible and avoiding certainty; the asserted universal numerical probability is not adopted.

Similarly, his fair-coin streak illustration and account-depletion examples ([45:40–48:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=2740s), [58:47–1:00:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=3527s)) are not a risk-of-ruin model for dependent, changing market outcomes with costs and discrete contract sizing. The size of a possible uninterrupted losing streak is not the whole account-survival problem.

The idea of measuring execution consistently is valuable without accepting every causal claim that psychology dominates all results. A well-executed negative-expectancy strategy still loses. The [FT71 statistics review](video-ft71-statistics.md) complements this note by separating event incidence, context and a defined executable opportunity.

## Boundaries across sources and situations

FT71's hypothetical margin amounts, instructional account sizes, historical percentage-risk examples and client target settings are separate from Vern's stated personal loss limits. His discussion of rebuilding confidence after a rough patch also differs from a permanent profit quota: he sometimes stops after a few well-executed trades for recovery, while ordinarily following opportunities within risk limits ([q_d, 1:30:02–1:33:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=q_dYsUnoiLI&t=5402s); [6q, 1:33:55–1:36:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6qkzPYIxonI&t=5635s)).

The later webinar initially asks a struggling trader to keep management fixed, then describes experienced traders making small target adjustments. Those describe different stages of skill and observation; they do not authorize arbitrary mid-sample changes. A robust research specification would separately define any permitted adjustment and retain every deviation.
