# Order-flow review: transactions, response and conditional execution

Sources: the three videos embedded in [T09 — Order Flow Review](topics/t09-order-flow-review.md). All retained lines of the complete retrieved English auto-caption exports were reviewed on 2026-10-23, including introductions, demonstrations, promotional sections and Q&A. Audio and chart frames were not inspected. Chart locations, fills and outcomes described here are the speakers' narratives, not independently verified trade records.

These are external instructors selected by Vern. The common thread is comparing participation with price response at a useful location. Their timeframes, execution styles, platform settings and opinions remain separate.

## 1. No BS Day Trading narrator — iWwxMokC0F8

[Full video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8), retained span 00:00–1:33:57. The narrator names No BS Day Trading near the close; his personal name is not established in this export. He divides the lesson into ladder mechanics, decision advantages and execution-cost arithmetic. The ES example is the September 2016 contract and the platform is expressly in **simulation** for the demonstrations ([05:21–06:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=321s)). His preference for Jigsaw is a disclosed product preference.

### Know which number you are reading

Resting bid/offer quantities are aggregated willingness to trade at a quoted price, not an individual trader count. A displayed total could come from many orders or several orders from one participant ([07:56–09:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=476s)). A buyer who crosses the spread trades with an offer; a seller who crosses trades with a bid. The transaction columns record executed volume. Their incremental prints accumulate according to the tool's reset rules; a number changing from five to six represents one additional contract rather than a new six-contract trade ([16:47–18:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=1007s)).

He explicitly shows displayed quantity shrinking by **more** than executed volume: the difference can be cancellations rather than trades ([13:55–16:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=835s)). Later an offer falls dramatically without equivalent printing, followed by lifting to higher prices ([1:06:11–1:09:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=3971s)). **Abacus clarification:** distinguish last-trade movement, changes in the quoted market, executions and additions/cancellations; a generic statement that price moves “only” because of market orders should not erase his own cancellation examples. Aggressive executions can also originate from marketable limit orders.

The cumulative volume profile is a third object: total traded contracts at each price over a selected session, with the overnight/reset choice adjustable ([41:51–46:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=2511s)). His loose comparison with Market Profile should not be used to conflate TPO counts, session volume and rolling current trades.

### A support level becomes a question

He contrasts two hypothetical descents into chart support. In the weak case, sellers keep hitting bids, offers follow downward, and little buying hits back into the offers. A pre-existing support line is insufficient reason to buy; he may avoid the long or consider continuation instead ([57:34–1:03:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=3454s)). In the alternative, the descent attracts substantial buying responses and the lowest price is difficult to trade at all. That can support paying one tick higher for a long rather than waiting for an unfilled bargain ([1:03:09–1:06:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=3789s)).

The instructor acknowledges three possibilities at the location: buy, sell through it, or do nothing when the activity is choppy. There is no every-touch rule ([1:10:54–1:13:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=4254s)). Once long, a disappearance of bid support combined with renewed aggressive selling can justify an early small loss rather than waiting for a distant arbitrary chart stop. He acknowledges that rapid response can also be chopped up ([1:13:31–1:15:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=4411s)).

### Better price versus certainty of participation

A limit order joins a queue; fills depend on transactions and orders ahead. The simulated ladder demonstration explains this explicitly ([47:24–50:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=2844s)). If sufficient trading into the limit looks likely, the narrator favors saving a tick; if the market may leave without him, he accepts paying the spread. His annual savings example assumes one avoidable ES tick per day and a simplified forty-week year ([1:25:27–1:31:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=5127s)). This is arithmetic conditional on achieving the better fills, not empirical evidence that all such ticks were obtainable. Missed trades, queue priority and adverse selection remain material.

The platform's historical order-entry demonstration moves loosely between stop-limit and stop-market language ([52:03–55:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=3123s)); do not use it as current operational instructions. Multiple Treasury ladders are proposed as additional context, not a verified causal lead ([1:19:50–1:22:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iWwxMokC0F8&t=4790s)). Claims that repeated scalping can outperform a chart trade are illustrative instructor arguments, not a measured comparison.

## 2. Peter Davies with Mind Muscles Academy — gdQNAMTDHfM

[Full video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM), retained span 00:08–1:03:52. Peter Davies is introduced as Jigsaw's founder; the host is introduced by auto-captions as Rich. Historical product rankings, prices, discounts, compatibility and liquidity conditions are promotional context, not current verified facts.

### Location comes first, behavior confirms or rejects it

Davies defines order-flow analysis as watching orders and their impact on price, then checking whether the expected behavior actually develops at a chosen location ([02:31–03:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=151s)). His list of possible responses at support is more useful than the single word “absorption”:

- Larger sellers cease while smaller activity continues.
- Aggressive selling continues before, at and beyond the supposed support: the premise has no confirming behavioral change.
- Repeated probes attract little selling: sellers appear unwilling to transact at that price.
- Heavy selling trades without further downward progress: bids absorb the executions.
- Buying initiative begins and price moves up.

These are different conditions, not interchangeable buy signals ([04:52–07:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=292s)). Large prints that previously produced progress but now fail to do so can suggest vulnerable positioning. Davies explicitly says not to trade every such pause in no-man's-land; he wants an area selected from preparation, today's profile or an approximately normal swing extent ([17:01–21:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=1021s)). His short large-trade history window is his display preference, not a universal observation period.

### Executed flow receives priority over theatrical depth

Unusually large displayed orders near support/resistance indicate that the area may attract attention, not that it must hold. Davies watches whether reasonable-sized bids follow a long position, while remaining wary of abnormally large, potentially withdrawn bids ([23:59–27:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=1439s)). Quantities such as 1,500 and 3,000 contracts belong to the historical ES-liquidity examples, not present-day filters.

He describes a possible “flip”: large offers above coexist with absorbed selling at the bid, then offers withdraw and buying pushes up. He narrates both sides as the same trader and warns against blindly joining the maneuver ([27:41–28:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=1661s)). **Abacus boundary:** aggregate public depth and prints cannot establish that common identity or intent. The observable components are displayed liquidity, executions, replenishment, withdrawal and response; deliberate manipulation remains an interpretation.

### The reset defines the question

Davies' current-trade columns reset when price revisits a level, capturing **this visit**. His bid/offer profile accumulates the session until manually reset. His ordinary volume profile aggregates both sides and separately displays value area, POC and VWAP ([28:44–34:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=1724s)). He favors transient bid/offer imbalances for immediate trapping/response and total volume structure when returning later to an area. He argues that the old aggressor imbalance loses relevance once price has left and positions may have exited ([34:40–37:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=2080s)). This is a strong warning against treating an old footprint imbalance as a permanent directional force.

An example of several thousand contracts executing against a small replenishing offer is described as an iceberg. Joining that offer still requires enough future volume to fill the queue; a large visible queue can prevent the follower's fill ([38:16–40:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=2296s)). The observable inference is replenishing liquidity, not independently established ownership of one hidden parent order.

For management, Davies can reset his flow baseline on entry. A small adverse price fluctuation without strong opposing transactions is different from an adverse move accompanied by overwhelming selling. The latter can cancel his long premise; the former can be tolerated ([43:55–45:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=2635s)). A large sell imbalance with **no** price progress instead calls for the absorption interpretation: volume alone is not sufficient.

### Training and scope

He advises beginners to watch executed current trades first, in short focused sessions, before adding every tool. He often suggests learning trade management before reversal entries, and relaxing the microscopic focus once a trade has room ([13:21–14:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=801s), [47:20–49:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=2840s)). His claims about rapid learning and successful customers are vendor claims. Later discussion makes market speed and personal aptitude explicit and recommends comparing markets when the current ES liquidity regime feels too fast ([58:29–1:00:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gdQNAMTDHfM&t=3509s)). Historical VIX/liquidity thresholds are descriptions of that episode, not stable laws.

## 3. Davies/Jigsaw: participation and momentum — ypyhHqzJovo

[Full video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo), retained span 00:00–23:05. The narration identifies Jigsaw and a Peter contact; the conceptual teaching is consistent with Davies' separately named presentation above. Examples are narrated over chart/order-flow recordings, including explicit after-the-event voiceover ([12:52–13:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo&t=772s)).

### What changes a reversal thesis

The instructor describes an upward progression of movement, local high-volume balance, and further movement. A pause with high participation is not sufficient reason to fade: new shorts can lose as the trend resumes. Only after one of those accepted areas fails downward does he take a reversal thesis more seriously ([00:00–03:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo&t=0s)). The competing explanation is **range expansion**: the apparent failure may merely join adjacent volume areas into a larger balance and precede hours of rotation ([03:30–04:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo&t=210s)).

His low-participation idea is explicitly about a lack of engagement **right now**, not an LVN from yesterday or last week. A retest toward the top of a prior accepted area may attract no willing sellers, supporting continuation ([04:37–08:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo&t=277s)). That is a behavioral observation distinct from the historical histogram location.

Momentum entry pays some ticks for visible initiative. The instructor rejects a universal requirement to catch the exact turn: other traders add during a move, and the key is not entering as that momentum expires ([08:11–11:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo&t=491s)).

### Narrated crude-oil cases

In the reversal example, the desired condition is buying aggression through previously heavily traded prices. He is prepared to exit if sellers' activity starts producing downward ticks. Selling that fails to move price down is tolerated, because response matters. Only after the desired upward impulse does he relax his immediate scrutiny. Near the top of an identified profile bulge, he resets the trade display, watches for renewed selling and exits rather than waiting for a return to entry ([11:40–17:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo&t=700s)). Auto-captioned crude prices are malformed; these exact numbers should not be reconstructed as verified levels.

The second trade is a **pre-breakdown momentum entry** toward an earlier liquidity/absorption area. He requires acceleration into the low, dominant selling and little opposing buying. After entry the expected event is sustained sell pressure and a prompt break; if buyers become dominant, he exits regardless of whether the small loss is two, three or four ticks ([17:28–19:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo&t=1048s)). A violent rapid break prompts him to exit quickly because he fears it may exhaust; a slower continuing churn would have made him more willing to hold ([19:40–20:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ypyhHqzJovo&t=1180s)). These are example-specific choices, not a fixed target or a rule to fade all acceleration.

## Tensions to preserve

**Abacus synthesis:** context narrows where to look; executed activity and price response distinguish continuation, absorption, exhaustion and nonparticipation; queue conditions determine attainable entry; fresh behavior can cancel the premise. A static LVN, a large print, a depth imbalance and a cancellation are four different observations.

The No BS narrator favors very rapid scratches and repeated scalps. Dalton elsewhere warns against frequent trades and emotionally tight stops. Davies combines fresh-flow exits with wider contextual location and cautions against overtrading. These are different horizons and styles; the knowledge base does not claim they are one coherent stop distance. Similarly, participant emotion and trapped-position stories can help pose hypotheses, but they must not replace observable evidence or imply every executed buy creates a new long. No video provides independently audited expectancy for the combined framework.
