NQ advanced AMD and the weekly decision process

Tmac's lesson is best understood as a hierarchy of decisions: form a conditional weekly view, identify the liquidity level that would make that view actionable, then wait for a local entry sequence. The chart pattern alone is not the whole method. Its most useful contribution is the distinction between having a bullish view and having an acceptable long entry; Thursday demonstrates that these can diverge. This is a qualitative reading of one selected week, not evidence of a profitable or reliably predictive system.

What the framework means

In the lesson, AMD means accumulation, manipulation, and distribution. These are the speaker's interpretations of visible price behavior; candles alone do not establish deliberate manipulation by an identifiable market participant.

Higher-timeframe context is used to choose which local patterns deserve attention. The presenter reads the preceding weekly reaction as bullish and initially aims toward the all-time high. A bearish intraday sequence can therefore be a pullback inside that larger view. His preference is to wait for the downside objective to be cleared before seeking longs, rather than treating every small reversal as a new weekly direction. This is a conditional ranking of setups, not a finding that countertrend trades cannot work. Source 2:33–6:21.

The sequence that carries across days

The same logic is nested across timeframes. A higher-timeframe sweep establishes a candidate directional turning point. A subsequent reaction and structure break make a local zone relevant. A later revisit provides a possible entry, and the resulting expansion can leave a new zone for the next day's analysis. The previous day's chart therefore changes the next day's context; each session does not begin with a blank slate.

The speaker also describes a bullish candle through an open–low–high–close path. That supplies a narrative for seeking upside after a candidate low. Reviewer interpretation: knowing the eventual high and low of a completed candle is very different from identifying them in real time. The completed candle's shape cannot be used as an input available earlier in the week.

Wednesday explains why the second order block matters

The most useful entry distinction appears here. A first zone may be a valid area of interest while an external low remains vulnerable. Buying it automatically can lead to a stop-out before the desired move. A later zone is preferred after the first reaction and a clearer structure break provide additional evidence. The lesson is not a universal rule to buy the second rectangle on a chart; the reason for preferring it is the information that has arrived between the two setups. Source 14:14–15:50.

A practical reading checklist

The following is a reviewer synthesis, not a complete executable strategy:

  1. Identify the larger directional thesis and its still-relevant destination.
  2. Mark the candidate external level using information already available.
  3. Wait for the hypothesized sweep and inspect the reaction; do not assume every breach is a reversal.
  4. Separate an aggressive sweep entry from a confirmed structure-break/retest entry. Decide which is being evaluated before seeing the outcome.
  5. Check the target, opposing higher-timeframe zones, repeated retests, and available reward relative to the chosen invalidation level.
  6. Pass when the structure or destination is unclear. Reassess when price fails the thesis or new information changes the context.

The video does not fully define step 6's invalidation or the position-sizing, stop-buffer, time-stop, scaling, and news-execution rules. Those gaps prevent faithful mechanical replication from this lesson alone.

What is demonstrated and what remains open

Demonstrated by the source: a coherent explanation of how the presenter links weekly context, local sweeps, confirmation, targets, and abstention in selected examples. It contains distinctions between possible and preferred entries, and between the broad objective and the actual stopping point.

Not demonstrated: a hit rate, calibrated probability, net expectancy, a complete trade log, or superiority to a simpler trend-and-pullback rule. The embedded clips at 1:58–2:21 and 13:22–14:00 are presented as earlier commentary, but this edited video does not independently authenticate original publication times or disclose all contemporaneous forecasts. Its opening perfect-prediction claim is therefore not an audited result.

Several supposedly separate confirmations also derive from the same price path: a sweep, a reaction, a BOS, and an OB may be descriptions of overlapping evidence. Reviewer inference: counting them as independent probability multipliers would be unjustified. The discretionary selection of swing, timeframe, and target also creates room to relabel a failed pattern after the outcome. Thursday's abstention is useful pedagogically, but cannot supply the missing denominator of all skipped and failed setups across other weeks.

A causal event record would separately store the time each swing becomes known, sweep time, confirmation time, zone-availability time, first eligible fill, and subsequent outcome. Right-hand bars required to confirm a pivot cannot be treated as known at the pivot itself. Unfilled limit orders remain unfilled; bars touching both target and stop require finer data or an explicit ambiguity rule.
